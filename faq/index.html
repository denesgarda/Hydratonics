Ingredients & safety

Specific to what's in it and whether it's right for your body.

Citric acid is a known trigger for some people, independent of dose. It's in here as a pH buffer and to mask the metallic taste from the potassium and magnesium compounds, not for any nutritional reason, so there's no functional loss in skipping a formula that has it if it's a trigger for you. If this has happened to you with other supplements or foods before, that's worth mentioning to whoever you'd normally ask about it.

It's in the formula in a trace amount, just enough to round off the tartness from citric acid and the mineral notes from the electrolytes, not a real sweetener dose.

Worth knowing before you rule it out: most "monk fruit sweetener" products on shelves aren't pure monk fruit. Pure mogroside extract is so intensely sweet that it's impractical to use on its own, so most commercial blends cut it with a bulking agent, usually erythritol, and sell the mix under the monk fruit name. If you've had a bad experience with a monk fruit product before, there's a real chance what actually caused it was the erythritol or whatever else it was cut with, not the monk fruit itself. This formula uses pure Luo Han Guo extract standardized to 50% Mogroside V, nothing blended in.

That's not a claim that a genuine mogroside reaction is impossible. If you've had a reaction to a verified pure monk fruit product specifically, that's still worth taking seriously regardless of the amount here.

It's not there for taste or energy. Dextrose Monohydrate is dosed in a 1:1 molar ratio with sodium specifically to activate SGLT1, the gut's sodium-glucose co-transporter, which is the most efficient absorption pathway sodium has. Without it, a meaningful share of the sodium you drink just doesn't get absorbed as well.

What sets this apart

The philosophy behind the formula and how it compares to what's already out there.

Two things, mainly. First, this comes in tiers dosed to specific exertion levels instead of one formula for everyone regardless of how much they're actually sweating. Second, it's built to taste as neutral as the real minerals in it allow, rather than flavored to be enjoyable to drink. Flavor doesn't hydrate you; it's just easier to sell than something that actually works, so we didn't optimize for it.

"Organic" describes how the sugar was grown, not what it does once it's in the formula. Cane sugar is sucrose, a glucose and fructose molecule bonded together, and it doesn't activate SGLT1 the way free glucose does since it has to be broken down first. Dextrose is glucose already, which is exactly what the absorption mechanism needs, and using more of it than the 1:1 ratio calls for would just be added sugar with no functional purpose.

Sweat loss is not the same at every intensity. A single formula dosed for a heavy training session is more sodium than a light walk needs, and a formula dosed for a light walk won't replace what a heavy session actually loses. Each tier is calibrated to a specific output level instead of averaging across all of them.

The trace minerals in sea salt or Himalayan salt are present in amounts too small to matter functionally; what you're actually getting is sodium chloride with a story attached. Using purity-controlled sodium chloride means the sodium dose is exact and consistent from batch to batch, which matters when the dose itself is the point.

Sweat-loss research reports sodium, potassium, and other minerals in elemental terms, meaning the actual atom, not the compound it's bound to for the formula to work. Reporting compound weight instead would produce a bigger, more impressive-looking number without changing how much of the actual mineral you're getting, so we report the number the dosing is actually based on.

Because the goal isn't sweetness, it's rounding off tartness and mineral notes with as little downside as possible. Luo Han Guo extract's sweet compounds (mogrosides) mostly pass through the gut without being broken down into anything metabolically meaningful, current research hasn't turned up toxic byproducts, and it carries GRAS status from the FDA. At the trace amount used here, it also skips the aftertaste some people get from other zero-calorie sweeteners.

That's not a claim that it's universally side-effect-free; individual reactions happen with any ingredient. But among sweetener options, it has about as clean a safety profile as the category gets, which is a real part of why it's the one used here.
